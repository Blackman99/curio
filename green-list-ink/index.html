What is this?

Before each word, the model hashes the word it just wrote together with a secret key. The hash seeds a random split of its 750-word vocabulary into a green list (γ of the words) and a red list. Then it adds δ to the green words' logits and samples. The letter still reads like the model's own writing, but it ends up with far more green words than chance would give.

The lamp is the detector. It knows the key, rebuilds each word's list from the word before it, and counts the greens. With T tokens after the first (words and punctuation both count) and g of them green, z = (g − γT) / √(Tγ(1−γ)). Human writing lands near 0. The paper flags z above 4: text written without the key gets that high about once in 31,600 tries (somewhat more often if it keeps repeating the same word pairs, which this toy model does).

The watermark is weak where the model had no real choice. If only one word can follow, δ cannot move it, and that word is green only by chance. That is why short or formulaic text is hard to watermark. Paraphrasing attacks it the other way: every word you change re-rolls its own colour and the colour of the next word, whose list it seeds.

Real: the watermark and the detector, as in the paper's soft red-list algorithm with one token of context, computed live. Toy: the language model is a word-level trigram (mixed 9 : 1 with a bigram) trained on about 4,000 words of seaside letters written for this exhibit, so it rambles. Its tokens are whole words and punctuation, not subword pieces; it won't repeat a four-token phrase, and its greeting and sign-off come from small tables of their own. "Paraphrase" just swaps random words for others the model allows between their neighbours; real paraphrasers are much smarter.

After Kirchenbauer, Geiping, Wen, Katz, Miers & Goldstein, A Watermark for Large Language Models (ICML 2023). The key is the one in their reference code.

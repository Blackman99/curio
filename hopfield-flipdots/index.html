What is this?

A Hopfield network (John Hopfield, PNAS 1982): 960 two-state units, one per disc, every pair joined by a symmetric wire of weight wij. Nothing else is stored. Pictures live in the weights.

Recall. Discs are visited one at a time in a fresh random order each sweep. A disc adds up every other disc's state times the wire between them, and turns yellow if the sum is positive, black if negative. Each flip lowers the energy E = −½ Σ wij si sj, so the board must stop somewhere: a valley. A sweep with no flips ends the run.

Hebb's rule wires the pictures in by adding them up, wij = Σ ξiξj. It assumes the pictures look nothing alike. For random pictures this board could hold about 0.138 × 960 ≈ 132 (Amit, Gutfreund & Sompolinsky, 1985); measured here, a stored random picture left to settle stays 99.9% intact on average with 100 stored, 99.2% at 132, and only 69% at 180, little better than a coin toss. These drawings share a dark background, so they interfere much sooner: Hebb keeps both pictures of a pair stable in 66 of the 66 pairs, all three of a trio in 163 of 220, all four in 4 of 495, and five or more in none (0 of 792 fives). The board then settles on spurious states, valleys between the memories that nobody stored, and the real pictures stop being valleys at all.

The projection rule (Personnaz, Guyon & Dreyfus, 1985) first takes out what the pictures share, W = Ξ C⁻¹ Ξᵀ / N with C their overlaps. Every stored picture is then exactly a valley. With all twelve stored it brought every picture back from 30% scrambled in 240/240 tries, and from 40% in 237/240.

All of it is computed in your browser: the weights, every update and the energy. Each picture's photo-negative is a valley too (flip every disc and nothing in E changes). The sign runs one sweep a second so you can watch; the arithmetic itself takes milliseconds. The clatter is synthesised noise, one click per flip, thinned out when hundreds flip at once.

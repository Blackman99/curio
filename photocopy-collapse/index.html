What is this?

The copier is a small generative model. To make a copy it fits itself to the page in front of it: how common each species is (a categorical distribution), and for each species the mean and spread of five traits (wingspan, hue, lightness, wing shape, how strong the markings are). The spread is the usual unbiased variance; one specimen alone gives zero spread. Then it prints 48 new butterflies by sampling that model. They are new samples, not copies of the drawings. The next copy is fitted to this copy, and so on.

A species that makes up a share q of the page is missing from the next copy with probability (1 − q)48, and once it is missing the model gives it probability zero, so it can never come back. Rare species go first. In 4,000 runs from this plate, the median copy at which each species first disappeared was: Luna moth (2 of 48) 5, Swallowtail (3) 8, Peacock (4) 12, Blue Morpho (6) 18, Cabbage White (9) 30, Brimstone (10) 34, Monarch (14) 52. Every run ended with a single species, after a median of 72 copies (quartiles 49–106). Which species survives is a lottery weighted by how common it was: Monarchs, 29% of plate 0, survived in 28% of runs, and Luna moths, 4%, in 4.5%.

Inside each species the spread is re-estimated from a finite sample every time. Each estimate is right on average, but the errors compound, and the spread drains away: variety was a median 70% of plate 0 after 10 copies, 40% after 40, 20% after 100 and 3% after 300. The averages drift too, so the last clones need not look like a typical original. This is the late stage of model collapse: low variance, and little resemblance to where it started.

What helps. Fresh specimens from the wild (drawn from the real population, red pins) put back what the copier forgot. With 5 of 48 per copy (10%), runs still had a median of 6 species and 101% of the variety after 300 copies; with 2 of 48, 5 species and 90%; with 12 of 48, all 7 and 103%. Learning from every page so far instead of the last one also holds: 7 species and 89% variety after 300 copies, because plate 0 never leaves the training data. What hurts: sharper sampling. At temperature 0.9 the median run was down to one species after 23 copies, at 0.8 after 13, and the commonest species won more often (49%, 64%). Text generators often sample below T = 1, or cut off the unlikely tail (top-p), which does the same.

Real: the fitting, the sampling and every number on screen, computed live from one seeded random stream (mulberry32); a separate Python implementation reproduces every copy, and the statistics above come from 2,000 to 4,000 runs per setting of a vectorised version of it. Simulated: the butterflies. They are drawings made from the five traits, styled after real species, and the "wild" is a made-up population. The tour uses a fixed random stream.

After Shumailov et al., AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data (Nature, 2024), which proves the single-species version (a Gaussian refit to its own samples loses its variance with probability 1) and shows that in the discrete case the survivor is drawn with its original probability. Keeping data: Gerstgrasser et al., Is Model Collapse Inevitable? (2024). Fresh data: Alemohammad et al., Self-Consuming Generative Models Go MAD (ICLR 2024). The species shares follow the same maths as gene frequencies on a small island (Wright–Fisher drift).

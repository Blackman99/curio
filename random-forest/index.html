What is this?

The meadow is a 2-D dataset: 200 training pebbles of two kinds, scattered around two shapes (two moons by default; their positions are generated from a seeded random number generator, not measured from anything). A further 2,000 fresh pebbles from the same recipe stay hidden and are used only for scoring.

Each tree is a real CART tree (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen & Stone, 1984) grown in your browser. It starts from a bootstrap sample: 200 draws with replacement from the 200 pebbles, so it sees about 63% of them, some two or three times. Every fork asks one question, x ≤ t? or y ≤ t?, choosing the threshold that leaves the two sides purest (lowest Gini impurity); a branch stops at a pure leaf or at the depth limit. In a random forest (Breiman, 2001) each fork may look along only one feature drawn at random (√2 rounded down); with “both” it is plain bagging (Breiman, 1996).

A tree votes with the majority of the training pebbles in the leaf a spot lands in; the forest votes with the majority of its trees (ties go to amber). The meadow’s tint is the share of trees voting jade; the pale line is where the majority flips.

Out-of-bag accuracy asks each training pebble only of the trees that never saw it. ρ̄ is the correlation between two trees’ mistakes on the fresh pebbles, averaged over every pair: Breiman showed a forest’s error is held down by strong trees whose mistakes are not alike. Across 30 two-moon meadows, one-feature trees score 90.7% alone against 92.3% for trees that see both, but their mistakes are less alike (ρ̄ 0.37 against 0.48), and the two forests of 60 end level at 95.2% and 95.1%.

Everything you see scored is computed: the trees, bootstraps, votes and accuracies, with the same arithmetic as a Python reference implementation that reproduces them bit for bit. The trees’ shapes are drawn from the real splits (a branch’s thickness grows with how many pebbles pass through it, its length only with depth); the falling leaves are decoration.

What is this?

A murmuration simulated live on your GPU. Each starling is a point with a velocity. Every frame, a chain of WebGPU compute shaders does the whole step for all 262,144 birds. First it rebuilds a spatial hash with a counting sort: each bird counts itself into a grid cell with an atomic add, a prefix sum turns the counts into offsets, and the birds are copied into cell order. There are four grids, with 2, 8, 32 and 128 m cells. Then every bird searches outwards, ring by ring, until its 7 nearest neighbours are certain. Last, it steers by them and moves. Nothing is precomputed or recorded.

The rules are Reynolds’ (1987): keep clear of birds closer than 1.1 m, match your neighbours’ velocity, drift towards their centre. On top of those: cruise near 12 m/s, prefer level flight, stay between 28 and 120 m up, turn back when more than 130 m from a roost point that wanders over the pier, flee a falcon within 20 m, and a small random jitter. The weights are mine, tuned until the flock looked like a murmuration. The birds are drawn 1.45 times life size (a starling spans about 40 cm) so you can see them, and each one banks into its turns, which is what makes the flock flicker as it wheels.

Seven nearest, not everyone close by. Ballerini et al. (2008) reconstructed flocks of up to 2,600 starlings over Rome in 3D. Each bird’s interaction reaches a fixed number of neighbours, nc = 6.5 ± 0.9, and that number stays the same whether the flock is dense or sparse; a fixed distance would not. Their 2D simulation (200 particles) showed why it matters: hit by a predator, flocks with a metric rule most often broke into 5 pieces, with stragglers making up 43% of the pieces, against 5% for the topological rule.

We ran the same test in 3D on 30 flocks of 262,144 birds, each starting compact and flying one way, as Ballerini’s did: 10 s of calm flight, then a falcon spawned 140 m out and dived through the centre for 8 s and left. We counted pieces 20 s after it appeared, with Ballerini’s link distance of 3 rc = 4.05 m. For the metric rule, rc = 1.35 m is about the 7th-nearest distance in the calm flock (median 1.23 m). Before the attack, metric birds actually had more neighbours in range (median 11). Metric flocks ended in a median of 197.5 pieces (89–503) with 87 lone stragglers (31–204); topological flocks in 43.5 pieces (13–106) with 1 (0–3; none at all in 13 of 30). Every one of the 30 metric flocks had more pieces and more stragglers than its topological twin. Lone birds were 38% of the metric pieces and 2% of the topological ones. Both rules lost a similar handful of large groups (about 2,000 birds outside the largest piece). Over the pier the falcon chases a single bird instead, because a folded flock’s centre can be empty air.

Why this needs the GPU. The CPU switch runs the very same step in JavaScript on the same birds, sliced across frames so the page stays responsive. On an Apple M4 Pro, one step for 262,144 birds took 3.3 ms on the GPU and about 1,000 ms in JavaScript. WebGL could draw the birds, but it has no compute shaders, atomics or storage buffers, so it cannot rebuild the hash on the GPU. The GPU and JavaScript versions find identical neighbour lists, checked against brute force, and agree on every position and velocity to within float32 rounding. They differ only where the GPU fuses a multiply and an add.

What is made up: the steering weights, the wandering roost, the falcon (it picks one bird and chases it with a limited turn; in the experiment it aims at the flock’s centre) and the scenery. The pier is an invented ruin. The neighbour search, the counting of pieces and every number in the panel are computed live.

Browser support (MDN, 9 November 2026): WebGPU is in Chrome and Edge 113+ on Windows, macOS and ChromeOS (Linux from 144, on Intel Gen12+), Chrome for Android 121+, Safari 26, and Firefox 141+ on Windows and 145+ on Apple-silicon Macs (not Linux or Android yet). Elsewhere the page falls back to the same step in JavaScript with 2,048 birds, drawn on a 2D canvas.

Sources: M. Ballerini et al., Interaction ruling animal collective behavior depends on topological rather than metric distance, PNAS 105, 1232 (2008); C. W. Reynolds, Flocks, herds and schools: a distributed behavioral model, SIGGRAPH 1987; MDN, WebGPU API.

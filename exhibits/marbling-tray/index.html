What is this?

Ebru is the Turkish art of paper marbling (on UNESCO’s list of intangible heritage since 2014). Paint mixed with ox gall is dropped on a bath of size thickened with gum tragacanth, where it spreads into floating discs. Styluses and combs drawn through the size drag the paint into patterns, and a sheet of paper laid on top lifts them off as a mirror image. The tour makes four classic ones: battal (stone), gel-git (come and go), nonpareil and bouquet.

The maths is from Shufang Lu, Aubrey Jaffer, Xiaogang Jin, Hanli Zhao and Xiaoyang Mao, “Mathematical Marbling” (IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 2012), with the formulas as Jaffer gives them on his Mathematics of Marbling and Dropping Paint pages. Each drop, comb and swirl is a closed-form map of the plane onto itself. A drop of radius r at c moves every point to c + (p−c)·√(1 + r²/|p−c|²). A tine moves paint along its track by z·ud, where d is the distance from the track; here u = 2−1/c, so the pull halves every c cm. A swirl does the same around a circle. A wavy comb is a straight comb between a sideways sine shift and its inverse, Jaffer’s trick for serpentine and bouquet patterns. Each map keeps every area exactly as it was.

The picture is drawn backwards. For every pixel the GPU runs the steps in reverse, asking where that point was before each one, until it lands inside a drop at the moment it fell. That is why the loupe stays sharp at any zoom. The outlines are pushed forwards. On the CPU, in double precision, each drop’s circle is pushed through every later step, and a chord is split, from the drop’s own circle, whenever it grows longer than 0.6 mm or bends sharply. That is the ink ledger, the traced drop and the SVG print. The two agree: on a grid of 106,800 points over the default bouquet, the GPU’s float32 answer and the float64 answer differ at 5, each within 0.01 mm of an outline.

What the ledger measures on the default bouquet: 158 drops become 2,100,194 vertices. Their outlines fell as 22.3 m of circles and are now 665.6 m long, 30× longer. Each paint’s area is what it was when it fell, to within 0.0009% (the worst single drop is off by 0.019%). That error is the chords cutting corners: with 1.2, 0.6 and 0.3 mm chords the worst of the 40 smallest drops is off by 0.052%, 0.019% and 0.0037%. Running the four strokes backwards, with −z, returns every point to within 7.8×10−13 cm, after moving them up to 6.6 cm.

Real and not. The maps are the model’s idealised steady state of a thin, incompressible, laminar layer. They are not a fluid simulation. The animation between “before” and “after” just scales each stroke, the bath is endless (paint pushed past the rim still counts in the ledger), and the darker rims and grain of the paint are shading. A Python reference reproduces all 158 outlines bit for bit, and every vertex mapped back lands on its drop’s circle to within 2.1×10−11 of its radius.

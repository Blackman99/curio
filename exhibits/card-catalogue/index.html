What is this?


A card catalogue of all 123 people who have won the Nobel Prize in Literature, 1901 to 2026, one pop-art index card each. Yellow cards were born in Europe, cyan in the Americas, white in Asia, black in Africa. Four filing orders: A–Z by surname, Year as a shelf of spines (each as tall as the writer was old at the prize), Born and Died stacked on a world map.


The page never animates a card. Changing the filing order rewrites the cards' positions in the DOM (in A–Z they sit in an ordinary wrapping flex row; in the other three they are absolutely placed), and that rewrite is handed to document.startViewTransition(). The browser photographs every card that has a view-transition-name (here card-<Nobel id>) before and after, builds a tree of pseudo-elements — ::view-transition-group(card-…) holding an old and a new snapshot — and animates each group from the old box to the new one while it crossfades the two snapshots. The only thing the page adds is timing: a per-card delay so the cards leave in a wave (A to Z, year by year, or west to east from the birthplace), and a playback rate for the slow-motion buttons. Both are set on the browser's own animations through the Web Animations API once transition.ready resolves.



Why it needs view transitions


None of these moves is something CSS transitions can do: a card going from a line of flowing text to an absolute spot on a map, letter tabs that exist in one order and not in another, an element whose writing-mode flips. A JavaScript "FLIP" animation could fake it within one page by measuring all 123 cards before and after. It cannot follow the cards across a page load. ↻ As a page load navigates to a brand-new document — the old page's scripts and DOM are gone — and both documents opt in with @view-transition { navigation: auto }, so the browser carries the snapshots across and the cards still fly. The new page reads the pagereveal event to add its own wave. The A–Z wave you see on first load is a transition too, from a shuffled deck.



X-ray


Switch X-ray on and pick an order: the flight freezes at take-off and the slider sets currentTime on every one of the browser's animations, so you scrub the real thing. Old snapshots get a dashed outline, new ones a solid one. During an ordinary document-wide transition the browser sends every pointer event to the root, so nothing on the page can be clicked until the flight ends. That is why the x-ray flight runs in a transition scoped to the card board, board.startViewTransition() (Chrome 147 and later): the rest of the page, slider included, stays live. Without scoped transitions X-ray plays the flight at 1/12 speed instead.



What it costs (measured)


    

  • For every flight in the test run, the browser's keyframes for all 123 groups match the boxes the page measured before and after the change: widths and heights within 0.01 px. Positions are rounded to whole pixels — snapshots are placed on the pixel grid.
    • 

  • Chrome 153 on an M4 Pro: median frame 17 ms during flights (60 fps; 90th percentile 20–33 ms), at device pixel ratio 1 and 2. Take-off costs one long frame of 160–200 ms; ready resolves 45–82 ms after the call. In software rendering (SwiftShader) the median frame is 17–50 ms.
    • 

  • The cost grows faster than the number of cards. On a bare test page with plain named boxes: 100 boxes 17.3 ms a frame and 29 ms to ready; 200 boxes 49.5 ms and 94 ms; 400 boxes 123.4 ms and 360 ms, and a 2-second flight took 5.5 s. A trace at 200 boxes spent most of each frame recalculating the style of the pseudo-element tree. That is why this catalogue is one prize, not all six.
    • 

  • Scoped transitions measured slower here (90th percentile up to 62 ms, the A–Z flight at a 43 ms median), so only X-ray uses them.
    • 

  • Jamming the main thread for 800 ms mid-flight: the screen kept getting new frames (40 of them) only because the snapshot crossfades run on the compositor; with crossfades switched off, 0 new frames. The card movement itself waits for the main thread.
    •

The data


Everything comes from the Nobel Foundation's own API (api.nobelprize.org v2.1, CC0), fetched 20 November 2026, including the prize motivations on the pulled-out cards. Places are the API's present-day names. The API leaves a few places blank or without coordinates: Abdulrazak Gurnah's birthplace (Zanzibar, from his Nobel biography), Eyvind Johnson's and John Galsworthy's birthplaces, and five deathplaces; their coordinates are from Wikidata. Ages are counted on 10 December of the prize year, the ceremony. Boris Pasternak (1958, made to decline) and Jean-Paul Sartre (1964, declined) are on the list, as the Foundation keeps them. The map is a plain latitude–longitude grid of land from Natural Earth (1:110 m, public domain), one dot per 1.2°.

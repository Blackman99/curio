What is this?

The ground is a real function f(x, y), drawn with height on a log scale (the contour lines are steps of ×√10 in f). The flag stands on its lowest point; a marble finishes when it comes within 1.5% of the course's width of a flag.

Each marble is one optimizer, and nothing else moves it: no gravity, no friction. At every step it reads the exact gradient g = ∇f under it and moves by its rule, in double precision:

SGD θ ← θ − η·g (Robbins & Monro, 1951).
Momentum v ← 0.9·v + g; θ ← θ − η·v, Polyak's heavy ball (1964), as PyTorch writes it.
RMSProp s ← 0.9·s + 0.1·g²; θ ← θ − η·g/(√s + 10⁻⁸), per coordinate (Hinton, Lecture 6e, 2012).
Adam m ← 0.9·m + 0.1·g; s ← 0.999·s + 0.001·g²; θ ← θ − η·m̂/(√ŝ + 10⁻⁸), with the bias corrections m̂ = m/(1−0.9ᵗ), ŝ = s/(1−0.999ᵗ) (Kingma & Ba, 2015).

Between steps the marble is drawn hopping from one point to the next; the arc is decoration, the landing points are the optimizer's. A marble that lands more than half a course-width off the table is out (“flew off”); one that hasn't finished after 1,500 steps is out of time.

Gradient noise adds σ·z to each component of g before the update, with z the sum of twelve uniform random numbers minus six (close to a standard normal), from a seeded generator, so a race replays identically. With σ = 0 SGD is plain gradient descent.

Courses: Rosenbrock's banana (1960), Beale's function (1958), Himmelblau's four valleys (1972), the saddle ½x² + ¼y⁴ − ½y², and a bowl with bumps, ½(x² + y²) + 0.6·(2 − cos 2πx − cos 2πy) (its cosines come from a 28th-order series, within 10⁻¹⁴ of the real thing, so a race replays bit for bit anywhere). The saddle and banana races echo Alec Radford's 2014 animations, which Sebastian Ruder used in An overview of gradient descent optimization algorithms (2016).

None of these is a neural network's loss. Two numbers stand in for millions, and which marble wins depends on the course, the gate and the learning rates: try SGD at η = 0.002 on the banana.

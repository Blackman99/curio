What is this?

The machine. A shoebox-sized bronze gearbox found in 1901 in a Roman-era shipwreck off Antikythera; 82 corroded fragments and 30 gears survive. X-ray tomography in 2005 recovered the gearing behind the main wheel and thousands of characters of inscription. This model follows Freeth et al. 2021: the surviving rear trains (Freeth et al. 2006, 2008) plus their reconstruction of the planetary Cosmos at the front, 67 gears in all. The crank’s 48-tooth crown wheel drives the great wheel b1 (223 teeth); one turn of b1 is one year, about 78.6 days per turn of the crank.

What is computed. Every pointer is moved by tooth counts and nothing else. Moon: 64/38 · 48/24 · 127/32 = 254/19 turns a year. Metonic spiral: 5/19 (235 months in five turns); Games dial ¼; Callippic 1/76. The e3 turntable turns 477/4237 a year (once in 8.88 years); the Saros spiral 940/4237 (223 months in four turns), the Exeligmos a twelfth of that. The front trains are those of Freeth et al.’s Fig. 3: Mercury 51 → 72 · 89 → 40 → 20 (1513/480), Venus 51 → 44 · 34 → 26 → 63 (289/462), Nodes 49 → 62 · 64 → 48 (−5/93, 18.6 years), Saturn 56 → 52 · 61 → 40 → 68 (427/442), Jupiter 56 → 64 · 45 → 40 → 43 (315/344), Mars 56 → 64 · 38 → 40 → 71 (133/284), the true Sun 56 → 52 → 56. The anomalies come from pins: the lunar pin sits 9.6 mm out on k1 and k2’s axle is 1.1 mm off (up to ±6.58°); the planets use the pin distances and offsets of their Supplementary Table S9; the Sun uses Hipparchus’ eccentricity of 1/24 with apogee at Gemini 5½°. Every meshing pair sits at (z1m1 + z2m2)/2 using the modules measured on the surviving gears.

Set on 23 August 205 BC. The Metonic calendar starts just after the new moon of 23 August 205 BC (Iversen 2017) and the Saros dial just after the new moon of 28 April 205 BC (Carman & Evans 2014). On that first date every pointer is set to the modern mean longitude of its body (Meeus; JPL). Choosing a date turns b1 by days ÷ 365.25, as if the Egyptian calendar ring were moved back a day every four years. The real sky comes from Meeus’ series for the Sun and Moon and Standish’s long-range elements for the planets, which I checked against JPL Horizons at 64 dates from 300 BC to AD 2100 (worst: Saturn 0.38°, Moon 0.019°).

How good was it? Over the first 19 years the Sun stays within 0.5° of the real Sun and the Moon within 3.3°; lock the pin and slot and the Moon strays 8.8°. The planets ride on uniform circles: Venus within 5.4°, Jupiter 6.5°, Saturn 8.1°, Mercury 12.0°, and Mars up to 33°, because a circle cannot follow Mars’s eccentric orbit. The Venus ratio 289/462 drifts 1.0° of synodic phase a century, a shade better than the Babylonians’ “exact” 720/1151 (1.1°) and far better than 5 cycles in 8 years (18.4°). Crank it to today and the Sun pointer is 16.8° behind: the 365¼-day year has gained 17.4 days on the seasons since 205 BC, the drift the Gregorian reform removed.

Eclipses. For each of the Saros dial’s 223 months the page finds where the Moon pointer meets and opposes the true Sun pointer, and marks the cell Η (solar) or Σ (lunar) if an end of the Dragon Hand is close enough. The limits I measured from NASA’s Five Millennium Canon for 300–100 BC: every solar eclipse came within 18.09° of a node and every umbral lunar eclipse within 11.96°. With those limits the dial flags 74 new and full moons in 55 cells; all 68 real eclipses of those 18 years (41 solar, 27 lunar) are among them, with 6 false alarms, and the mechanism’s new and full moons fall within 0.19 days of the real ones. With the tighter limits (16.67° and 10.38°) it flags 64 and none is false, but 4 are missed. The real dial has 51 glyph cells and predicted only what could be seen, which this does not attempt.

What is simplified. The gear positions are my own layout: every pair meshes at the right distance, but Freeth et al.’s front gears sit elsewhere, and they use 0.5 mm modules here. The Moon’s phase ball turns directly; the two gears of its differential are left out. The Dragon Hand is hypothetical, as Freeth et al. say. Month names: each year starts with the first month after the Sun returns to its starting longitude, and Eukleios is doubled in the seven 13-month years, Freeth et al.’s proposed intercalary month; the real sequence survives only in part. The front ring is drawn as the 365-day Egyptian calendar; counts of its holes suggest 354 (Budiselić et al. 2020). The glyphs here say only Η or Σ, not the hour. The parapegma text is the published transcription.

What is this?

The engine. A Chevrolet small-block 350, introduced in 1967. GM gives the main numbers: 4.000″ bore, 3.480″ stroke and a 9.025″ deck height, plus 4.4″ between bores, a 90° V and the firing order 1-8-4-3-6-5-7-2 (Wikipedia). The connecting rods are 5.700″ between centres (Manley’s catalogue). The cam is GM’s 10185071 hydraulic roller: 0.474″/0.510″ valve lift with 1.5 rockers, 208°/221° at 0.050″, 275°/280° advertised, 112° lobe separation. The stack checks out: half the stroke plus the rod plus a 1.560″ piston compression height leaves the crown 0.025″ below the deck.

What is computed. Every frame starts from one number, the crank angle. Each piston sits where the slider-crank formula puts it for its own crankpin and bank, and each rod hangs between the two pins. In the functional test I measure the 3D parts themselves: every big end sits on its crankpin and every small end on its wrist pin. The firing order is checked against the crank: the page lists every even-firing order a pin layout allows, and GM’s order is one of the 8 for pins at 0°, 90°, 270° and 180°. Each lobe is ground as the inner offset of the roller’s path, and the cam turns at half speed through 20- and 40-tooth sprockets, whose chain comes out at 55.0 links. The lobe shape is a raised-cosine power (p = 1.734 intake, 1.305 exhaust) that I fitted through GM’s three published numbers. GM doesn’t publish where the lobe is phased, so I assumed an intake centreline of 108° ATDC, the cam ground 4° advanced. At 0.050″ that opens the intake 4° after top centre and closes it 32° after bottom centre. The exhaust opens 46.5° before bottom centre and closes 5.5° before top centre. In the flat-plane version the page regrinds the cam: each lobe turns to the new firing order.

Why it burbles. The banks don’t take turns. The left bank fires cylinders 1, 3, 5 and 7 at 0°, 270°, 450° and 540°, so its pipe gets pulses 270°, 180°, 90°, 180° apart, and the right bank’s pipe is just as uneven. I checked every pin layout a cross-plane crank can have: all 48 even-firing orders leave each bank with gaps of either 90-180-180-270 or 90-90-90-450, never even. Counted in engine orders (times per crank turn), the left pipe’s pulse train has no 2nd order at all. Its strongest lines are 1.5, 0.5 (0.78 of the 1.5 line) and 2.5 (0.34), all at half the bank’s average rate. That uneven lope is the burble. With a flat-plane crank each pipe gets one pulse every 180°, so it carries only orders 2, 4, 6 and so on, and in Python the half orders come out at zero to 1e-17.

The sound. The exhaust is synthesised live in an AudioWorklet from the same timing. Each cylinder’s pulse follows its exhaust valve’s lift times a decaying blowdown, and the sound follows the rate of change of that flow. It then goes into a 2 m pipe that reflects inverted from its open end, and through a two-stage low-pass for the muffler. With true duals the left bank plays in your left ear and the right bank in your right. An X-pipe mixes the two. The pulse shape and the pipe are my simple models. The timing is the real engine’s. A Python port of the synth reproduces the page’s samples exactly: 108,000 stereo samples, including changes of speed, crank and pipe partway through.

Shaking. The block feels minus the mass times the acceleration of every piston, plus the pull of the crankpins and big ends. I estimated stock part weights: 1.01 kg reciprocating per cylinder, 2.49 kg turning with each throw. The page uses the exact slider-crank acceleration. At 6,000 rpm the flat-plane engine shakes sideways with up to 31,203 N, almost all of it second order, which counterweights can’t cancel. Its second-order line is 4√2·m·r·ω²·A₂ = 31,172 N, where A₂ = 0.3127 is the exact second-order coefficient (λ = 0.3053). The cross-plane crank without counterweights rocks end to end with up to 15,363 N·m, against the textbook √10·m·r·ω²·d = 15,354 N·m; the difference comes from the left bank sitting half a rod ahead. With counterweights on every web all that remains is a 763 N fourth-order vertical shake (4√2·m·r·ω²·A₄, A₄ = 0.00764) and 9 N·m from that stagger. An independent Python version agrees with the page to 1e-7. It finds the pistons by vector closure and differentiates numerically.

What is simplified. Anything GM doesn’t publish is my estimate from photographs: the shapes of the heads, manifold and pan, the valve gear geometry, the 4.52″ between crank and cam, the lifter angle and the part weights. The counterweights are drawn as a full set, one opposite each pin; production 350 cranks use six weights placed differently. The valves move by exactly 1.5 times the lifter rise because I made the rocker geometry linear. The spark advance (8° at idle to 34° by 3,200 rpm) and the glow in the cylinders are illustrations. Above 300 rpm the picture is slowed down, or it would just flicker. The movement on the stand is exaggerated and simply follows the unbalanced force.

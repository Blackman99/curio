What is this?

The pendulum. Two identical uniform rods, the upper one hung from a fixed pivot and the lower one from the end of the upper, as in Jeremy Heyl's The Double Pendulum Fractal (UBC, 2008). Both start at rest at the pixel's angles: θ₁ across, θ₂ up, each from −π to π. The page integrates Heyl's equations of motion (his eqs. 7–10, in momentum form, with m = l = g = 1) by the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with a fixed step of 0.01 √(l/g) (Heyl used adaptive steps), for up to 20,000 steps: t = 200 √(l/g). A pixel's colour is the first time either rod passes straight up (|θ| > π). Colours follow his Fig. 2: green within 10, red within 100, purple after (his purple runs to 1,000, ours stops at 200); within each colour, darker flips sooner. White pendulums had not flipped by t = 200. The ivory lens inside the curve 3 cos θ₁ + cos θ₂ = 2 cannot flip at all: those pendulums start with less energy than it takes to lift the lower rod over the top (Heyl's eq. 13), so nothing is integrated there.

The whole map. At 512 × 512: 262,144 pendulums, 63,924 inside the lens. Of the other 198,220, in double precision, 133,288 flip before t = 10, 49,872 between 10 and 100, 3,368 between 100 and 200, and 11,692 not by 200. Against Heyl's Fig. 2, sampled at the same 512 × 512 angles: where his colour is unambiguous, 99.7% of our green pixels are green in his figure, and 92.1% of his red ones are red here.

Two computers. The map runs on your GPU in single precision (32-bit floats, 24 bits of significand): a WebGL2 fragment shader keeps every pendulum's angles and momenta in a floating-point texture and takes up to a few hundred steps per frame. Sine and cosine are not the GPU's own; the shader evaluates the same Taylor polynomials as the CPU. Meanwhile your CPU (Web Workers, one per core) redoes every pendulum in double precision (53 bits), every eighth pixel first, then every fourth, every second, and the rest. “Where they differ” marks pendulums whose first flip comes out more than one step (0.01) apart, or that flip in one and not in the other.

What differs. Single against double precision, both on the CPU, over the whole map: 8,946 of 198,220 pendulums (4.5%). Of the 133,288 that flip before t = 10, 4 differ; the first difference is at t = 8.11. Of those that flip between 10 and 20, 0.9% differ; 30–50, 21.8%; 50–100, 52.8%; 100–200, 88.5%. A GPU is not quite IEEE single precision either: it fuses a·b + c into one rounding. On the machine this page was built on (Apple M4 Pro), the GPU and the CPU, both in single precision, give the same flip step for 187,332 of the 198,220 pendulums and differ by more than a step for 7,916 (4.0%), none of them before t = 10.1: nearly as many as single against double.

Each bit buys a fixed amount of time. Put a twin beside each pendulum that rounds every number to p bits (24 bits is exactly IEEE single; other widths round each double-precision result with Veltkamp's split) and time when the two are first 0.1 rad apart. Median over 12,384 pendulums (a 128 × 128 map without the lens): 8.8 at 12 bits, 30.4 at 24, 73.0 at 48. From 12 to 48 bits that is a straight line, 1.78 time units per bit (no point more than 0.25 off it). Rounding errors grow e-fold every 2.6 time units (a Lyapunov exponent of 0.39 per √(l/g)), so halving the error buys a fixed amount of time, not twice the time. Double precision's 29 extra bits over single buy about 52 more; the line points to about 82 at 53 bits, which this page cannot check: that would need a still wider arithmetic as referee. At 8 bits the twins part almost at once (0.63).

Neither is the pendulum. Both computers solve the same stepped equations, and the step has its own error. Double precision with step 0.01 against step 0.005: twins part at a median t = 36.8, as if the step were worth about 28 bits. Beyond t ≈ 36.8, the colours in the speckled band belong to neither the real pendulum nor any one arithmetic; only their statistics do. Heyl's finding that the edge of the stable set is a fat fractal, about 0.15% of all starting angles undecidable at any precision, is about the true pendulum, not about one computed picture.

Cross-checks. The double-precision, single-precision and p-bit arithmetics are written twice, in JavaScript for the page and in Python with numpy, with the same operations in the same order and their own sine and cosine (Taylor series after a Cody–Waite reduction), so neither language's maths library can make them differ: 6,140 flip times, partings and trajectory samples match bit for bit. In the tests, the page's own log (every ladder and twin, and a sample of the double-precision verdict) is replayed in Python and matches too.

What is simulated. Ideal rigid rods, frictionless pivots, no air. Over 200 random pendulums to t = 200, the step drifts the energy by at most 2×10−4 mgl. Below 24 bits the GPU rounds each single-precision result again, and the ghost twin rounds each double-precision result, so map and ghost can differ in the last bit there.

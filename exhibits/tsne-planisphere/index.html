What is this?

t-SNE (Laurens van der Maaten & Geoffrey Hinton, Visualizing Data using t-SNE, 2008) draws high-dimensional points on a plane so that points that were near stay near. For each star it puts a Gaussian over the other pictures, its width tuned by binary search until the distribution's perplexity (2 to the power of its entropy) hits the value you pick: that is how many neighbours a star listens to. On the chart, closeness is measured with a heavy-tailed Student-t curve instead, and the stars are moved by gradient descent to make the two sets of closeness agree, minimising the KL divergence between them.

The run here is exact (every pair, every step, no Barnes–Hut shortcut) and follows the paper's recipe: 1,000 steps from a speck of random dust (spread 0.0001), momentum 0.5 then 0.8 from step 250, per-coordinate gains (Jacobs, 1988), and early exaggeration, where the pull between neighbours is multiplied ×12 for the first 250 steps (the paper used ×4 for 50; ×12 is today's default). The learning rate is N/12, as Belkina et al. (2019) propose.

The digits are generated, not scanned: two ways of writing each digit, randomly slanted, stretched, bent, wobbled and inked with a pen of random width, then, like MNIST, scaled into a box, centred on their centre of mass and inked onto a 20 × 20 grid. Each picture's 400 pixel values are its coordinates. The colours and constellation names are painted on afterwards from the true digits; t-SNE never sees them. A digit you draw is placed at the median position of its 10 nearest pictures (as openTSNE places new points), then t-SNE runs 300 more steps on all the stars, without exaggeration.

Real: the pictures, the distances, the perplexity search, every step of t-SNE (double precision, own exp and log, a seeded generator, so a run replays exactly). Checked offline against an independent Python version, which reproduces runs bit for bit, and against a numerical gradient. Measured on this catalogue over 20 random starts at perplexity 30: 98% of each star's 10 nearest stars are the same digit (in 400 dimensions, only 94% of its 10 nearest pictures are), and about two-thirds of its 10 nearest pictures stay among its 10 nearest stars.

How to read it (Wattenberg, Viégas & Johnson, How to Use t-SNE Effectively): who sits next to whom inside a constellation means something. The size of a constellation and the gaps between them mostly don't, and at low perplexity t-SNE invents clumps. Up and down mean nothing either, which is why this chart turns.

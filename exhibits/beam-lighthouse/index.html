What is this?

The sea is a language model. It is a word-by-word model of Part One (“The Window”) of Virginia Woolf’s To the Lighthouse (1927): 2,064 sentences, 48,777 words and punctuation marks, 5,298 different ones. It is an interpolated Kneser–Ney trigram (Kneser & Ney 1995; Chen & Goodman 1998): the probability of each next word given the two before it, with absolute discounts D = n1/(n1+2n2) (0.888 for trigrams, 0.805 for the level below). Your browser counts its 39,513 distinct three-word sequences from the text when the page opens. The text is the Standard Ebooks edition, public domain in the United States; quotation marks are dropped.

The lamp has k beams. At each step every sentence still at sea is extended by its k most probable next words, and only the k most probable sentences of all are kept: one beam each. The rest sink. A sentence that reaches a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark moors (the buoy) and stays in the beam, competing with the rest; with no length penalty the search ends as soon as the most probable sentence in the beam has moored, because a sentence still at sea can only lose probability. Anything still going after 30 words reaches the horizon. One beam is greedy decoding. Under each word is its probability given the two words before it.

Exact summit runs Dijkstra’s algorithm over the model’s states (its last two words) with cost −ln P, until the cheapest unexplored state costs more than the best finished sentence. That sentence is then provably the single most probable continuation the model can write. It needs few states: median 8, at most 67, over 500 openings.

Measured over 500 openings (the first 1–5 words of random sentences from the book). One beam reached the horizon without ending 62.2% of the time, and 41.8% of its three-word phrases were repeats. As the beams grew, the average sentence found shrank: 20.6 words with 1 beam, 8.5 with 2, 3.4 with 4, 2.0 with 8, 1.5 with 16, 1.2 with 32. The share matching the exact summit rose from 6.8% to 44.4% (4 beams), 86.2% (16) and 100% (32). The summit itself was to stop at once, a full stop straight after the opening, 82.4% of the time; it averaged 1.2 words. Woolf’s own continuations average 25.6. Wider is not always better: for 19.4% of the openings some k+1 beams found a less probable sentence than k beams did (try The sea would with 2 beams, then 3).

Length penalty. The slider divides a sentence’s log probability by ((5+|y|)/6)α, the GNMT penalty (Wu et al. 2016). With 8 beams, α = 1 barely helps (2.3 words on average); at α = 1.5 the search tips back into loops (55.6% reach the horizon).

The same story in big models. Stahlberg & Byrne (EMNLP 2019) ran exact search on a Transformer translation model: “for more than 50% of the sentences, the model in fact assigns its global best score to the empty translation”. Holtzman et al. (ICLR 2020) found that “using likelihood as a decoding objective leads to text that is bland and strangely repetitive”, and proposed nucleus sampling, which the dice use by default: draw only from the most probable words that together make up 90%. Meister, Cotterell & Vieira (EMNLP 2020) note that beam search wins “despite its overwhelmingly high search error rate”, and argue that it does so by keeping information density even.

By dice sentences average 15.5 words (nucleus 0.9) or 15.8 (every word), at a geometric-mean 1 in 55 per word, against 1 in 11 for Woolf and 1 in 5.9 for 2 beams. The model has read every Woolf sentence it scores, so hers are flattered.

What is real. All of it runs live: the model, every beam, the exact search, the dice (mulberry32, seeded). A separate Python implementation reproduces the page’s searches, samples and scores bit for bit (747 random cases), with the same series for ln so the two agree to the last digit. It is a toy: a trigram remembers only two words, it has read only Part One, and words it has never seen can’t be typed. The beams, buoys and horizon are just the stopping rules above.

What is this?

What you are looking at

A darkroom under a red safelight. Between the negative and the paper sits a filter, and here the filter is a grid of numbers, a kernel. To print one pixel, the grid is laid over that pixel and its neighbours; every tone (0 is black, 1 is white) is multiplied by the number on top of it, the products are added up, the offset is added, and that is the pixel's tone on the paper. Paper can't be blacker than black or whiter than white, so anything below 0 or above 1 is clipped. Do the same at every one of the 262,144 pixels and you have a convolution. "One pixel, worked out" shows the sum for the pixel under your pointer.

Strictly, this is cross-correlation: the grid is not flipped before it is laid down. That is also what every convolutional neural network computes, and what they all call convolution.

What is computed and what isn't

Every print, camera frame and learning step is computed in this page, in JavaScript, in double precision. The developing animation (shadows come up before highlights) is a picture of the chemistry, not a model of it, and the red is a palette applied last: Download gives you the plain black-and-white print with its filter written in the margin. Camera frames never leave the page.

The learner

"Who made these?" gives a small network the negative and an old print, nothing else. 1 filter is one kernel and an offset, exactly like the enlarger's. 2, 4 or 16 + ReLU is that many kernels, each followed by a ReLU (negative values become zero), mixed by one weight each into a single print: a one-layer convolutional network. It learns by gradient descent on the mean squared difference between its print and the old one, with Adam (step size 0.02 for one filter, 0.01 with ReLUs), looking at 128 random pixels per step, 4,000 steps per press of Learn. The gradient passes straight through the paper's clipping. Starting filters are random; New start draws new ones. "Match" is the share of the old print's variance the network's print explains (R², on every 16th pixel).

Relief is one 3×3 filter, and one learned filter finds it: the learned numbers are within 0.002 of the owner's (median of 30 starts; worst 0.010).

Edges also matches, but the numbers come out different, by 0.398 at the median. Most of that print is clipped to black, and many different filters print the same black, so the print doesn't pin the filter down.

Solarized (1 − |2v − 1|, the Sabattier effect: a print flashed with light halfway through developing) folds the bright half of the tones back down. One filter is a straight line in every tone and cannot fold anything; the best it reaches is about 50.8%.

Line drawing (1 − 3 × |Laplacian|) marks an edge the same way whichever side is darker. A single filter has to answer one side with plus and the other with minus, so its best print is almost blank grey: 0.5%.

Two filters and a ReLU are enough for both, exactly: for the line drawing, a Laplacian and its negative, each rectified, then subtracted from white. Yet from random starts gradient descent found such a solution only 5 times in 30 for the line drawing and 5 for the solarized print. With 16 filters it was 26 and 30. Networks larger than they need to be are easier to train, an effect Livni, Shalev-Shwartz and Shamir also report; the 16-filter networks here don't find the tidy two-filter answer, they find some other mix that works.

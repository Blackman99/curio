What is this?

The machine. The Enigma I was the cipher machine of the German Army and Air Force in the Second World War; more than 20,000 were made. A key press first turns the rotors, then closes a circuit from a 4.5 V battery through the plugboard (Steckerbrett), the fixed entry wheel, three of the five rotors I–V, the fixed reflector (Umkehrwalze), back through the rotors and the plugboard, and into one of 26 lamps. The rotor wirings, turnover letters and reflectors A, B and C are from Crypto Museum’s wiring tables. Army rotors carry the numbers 01–26 on their index rings, and this model uses them too.

What is computed. Every press runs the real machine. The stepping follows Hamer (1997): each rotor has a 26-tooth ratchet on its right side and a notch on the left edge of its index ring. Lever 1 always pushes the right rotor. Lever 2 rides on the right rotor’s ring and drops into its notch, and then pushes the middle rotor. Lever 3 does the same on the middle rotor, and when it drops in it pushes the left rotor and the middle one with it, which is the double step. The notch sits eight letters past the turnover letter, where the levers are. The current is then traced contact by contact, and the glowing path in X-ray runs through the 3D contacts and wires it actually uses. The page’s engine and an independent Python implementation agree at every stage on 44,259 random key presses (300 machines, all reflectors, rings and up to 13 cables). They also pass the published checks: AAAAA → BDZGO (rotors I II III, reflector B, start AAA), the stepping ADU → ADV → AEW → BFX → BFY and Hamer’s own table, and the 162-letter example on German Wikipedia, which re-enciphers letter for letter.

A real message. Message Nr. 113 (group XIVFG) was received on 7 July 1941 and is part of the German Army collection published by Geoff Sullivan and Frode Weierud’s Breaking German Army Ciphers project. They give that day’s key as reflector B, rotors 2 4 5, rings BUL (02 21 12) and the cables AV BS CG DL FU HZ IN KM OW RX, with the message started at OPQ and stopping at OQQ. On this machine the indicator works out: from LMN, the letters JGO give OPQ. From OPQ the 26 letters of cipher text give WOBLEIBTZAHLENMELDUNGFRAGE (“wo bleibt Zahlenmeldung? Frage”), and the windows stop at OQQ, as listed.

The double step and the period. For every one of the 60 rotor orders the windows come back to where they started after 16,900 key presses, 26 × 25 × 26 rather than 17,576. Once typing starts, 676 of the window settings never come round again. You will also see 16,926 quoted as the period. I counted that as the number of different start positions, because 650 settings give the same text as another one.

Never itself. The reflector pairs every contact with a different one, so the current always comes back on another wire. The page tries all 456,976 settings and keys of the AAAAA-test machine and finds none that comes out as itself. In Python I tried all 60 rotor orders and the three reflectors, 82,255,680 encipherments in all, and found none either. Each of those substitutions is also its own inverse, which is why the same settings decrypt. Codebreakers used the first rule to slide a guessed word along a message and rule out any position where a letter would land on itself.

How many settings. Ten cables can be plugged in 150,738,274,937,250 ways. Eleven would give the most, 205,552,193,096,250. With 60 rotor orders and 17,576 start positions that is 158,962,555,217,826,360,000, all computed exactly. Crypto Museum notes that Welchman’s improvement to the bombe exploited the fact that cables swap letters in pairs.

What is simplified. The parts are modelled from Crypto Museum’s photographs of an Enigma I, and their sizes are my estimates. The levers rise straight up rather than swinging. The wiring from the keys and lamps to the plugboard is drawn schematically, while the path inside the rotors, the reflector and the entry wheel is the real one. A lamp stays lit for a moment after the key comes up.

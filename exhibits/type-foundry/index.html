What is this?

The sheet. William Caslon's specimen sheet, dated 1734, showed each size his foundry cast by setting the same speech in it: Cicero's first against Catiline, Quousque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra? (scan). The forme here sets ten of the sheet's sizes, French Cannon to Pearl, restarting the text at each size as the sheet does. The point sizes are the ones American founders later standardised, 48 pt down to 5 pt, at 1 pt = 4/3 CSS px. Caslon's compositors justified each line with the word spaces and hyphens. Here each line is justified with the font's width axis.

One file, every cut. The type is Roboto Flex (Font Bureau, David Berlow and others; SIL Open Font License). For this page it is cut down to Basic Latin and three of its thirteen axes: weight (100 to 1000), width (25 to 151) and optical size (8 to 144). The other ten axes are pinned at their defaults, and the cut has no kerning or ligatures. That leaves 138 KB, 108 KB gzipped inside the page. The browser draws all the type itself. Every letter is a real character in a span with its own font-variation-settings, so you can select, copy, search and read the specimen aloud.

What is computed. The page reads the font bytes the browser draws from with its own small parser, no library. It uses fvar and avar to normalise each axis value, gvar for the outlines and HVAR for the advance widths. In gvar each glyph carries 13 to 27 sets of point deltas, and points a set leaves out are interpolated (IUP). Checked against fontTools 4.68, 156,630 control points of all 108 glyphs at 69 locations agree to the last bit, and so do the advances. In the matrix, the orange outline is that computation drawn over the browser's own grey glyph. Compared pixel by pixel in Chrome 147 for 40 letters: at 120 px not one of the 95,354 pixels they cover differs by more than half its coverage, and at 257 px (the matrix on a 1440 × 900 screen) only 17 of 430,951 do. At 180 and 231 px Chrome draws the letters 4.3% and 4.2% heavier, with every difference within 1 px of our outline's edge.

How a line is justified. Each line gets one width value. On each side of the default, advances are linear in the normalised width, so the page bisects for it, then checks the exact sum against how this browser lays out text, which it calibrates on load. Chrome rounds every inline box up to 1/64 px and truncates font sizes to 0.01 px, so sizes here are multiples of 0.25 px. WebKit adds unrounded advances. Firefox rounds the HVAR deltas to whole font units, as HarfBuzz does. Repeated letters cross Chrome's 1/64 px steps together. So once the line's width is found, each letter's own width is moved by a few thousandths of a unit to the middle of its step, and single letters are widened one step until the line is within 1/64 px. Measured in Chrome 147 over 4,760 laid-out lines (the whole tour, the ladle, every measure from 55% to 100%, a phone and your own copy), every line's width equals the page's prediction and the measure exactly (0.000 px). Firefox 148 keeps them within 0.24 px and WebKit 26.4 within 0.02 px. Line breaks come from dynamic programming that keeps each line's width as close to normal as it can.

Optical size. Metal type was cut separately for each size, and small sizes were drawn sturdier and wider. In Roboto Flex at 8 px, relative to the size, the x-height is 19% taller, the stems 85% thicker and the word ‘minimum’ 33% wider than at 144 px. Every line here uses the cut for its own pixel size.

What is not real. The ladle is decoration. The glow and the swelling letters are only a way to show the axes moving. Squash, word spaces and letter-spacing are there for comparison. Browser support (MDN): variable fonts since Chrome 62, Firefox 62 and Safari 11; DecompressionStream, which unpacks the embedded font, since Chrome 80, Firefox 113 and Safari 16.4.

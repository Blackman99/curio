What is this?

A bucket that learns. In reservoir computing you do not train the network. You pour a signal into something with rich, fading dynamics (here a tank of water) and train only a linear readout that looks at its state. This tank is simulated: a 64 × 40 grid obeying the damped linear wave equation, with reflecting walls and 1% of each ripple's speed lost per tick. Sixteen dippers sit on the short walls. Every step each one dips in and back out by tanh(a·u + b) − tanh(b), with its own random a (1 to 6, either sign) and b (−2 to 2), and the water then runs 12 ticks. 273 floats on a 21 × 13 grid each report two numbers: their height h and their lamp, h². The readout is a weighted sum of those 546 numbers plus the signal itself and a constant: 548 weights, from one ridge regression (λ = 10⁻⁸ per sample, on standardised readings) over 2,000 steps after a 300-step warm-up. The water, the dippers and the floats are never adjusted.

Unplugged. Pull the plug and the guess becomes the next input, so the tank runs on its own. The signal is Mackey–Glass with delay τ = 17, read once per time unit and squashed by tanh(x − 1), the setup Jaeger and Haas used. Across 20 random tanks (seeds 1 to 20), unplugged right after training, the dream stays within 0.3 of the signal's spread for a median of 1,092 steps (762 to 1,650; seed 7, the default, 1,102). The error at the 84th step, root-mean-square over the 20, is 1.8×10−4 of the spread; Jaeger and Haas scored that step with a 1,000-neuron network trained on 3,000 steps. After it slips off the timeline, all 20 of the 20 keep drawing the same spread and range: it loses the future, not the shape.

The bends. Small waves add up linearly, so the water itself is a linear filter with a long, fading memory. The nonlinearity comes from two places, and you can take each away (medians over the same 20 tanks): lamps off, 742 steps; straight dippers, 31.5; both straight, 10; and with the tank drained, so the readout sees only the signal and its square, 1. Viscosity matters both ways: thin water that rings for long holds 432 steps, the default 1,092, syrup 408. The wilder τ = 30 holds 493.

Splashes. A splash is a poke in and out, so it adds no water. While the tank listens, its guess is back within 0.001 of an unsplashed twin's after at most 108 steps (median 90.5): the memory fades on its own, which is what makes it a reservoir (the "echo state" property). While it dreams, a splash at step 150 parts the dream from its unsplashed twin a median 115 steps later, and all 20 of 20 still look like Mackey–Glass.

A real bucket. In 2003 Chrisantha Fernando and Sampsa Sojakka put a glass tank of water on an overhead projector, drove small weights into it with motors on its sides, filmed the light at 5 frames a second, ran a Sobel edge filter with a threshold over each frame (their bend), and trained 50 perceptrons on a 32 × 24 grid. The bucket solved XOR on 85% of new frames and told a spoken "zero" from "one" with 1.5% training error, against 25% for the raw sound. They noticed the XOR solution used where the waves were, not their interference.

What is real. Every number on this page is computed in your browser in 64-bit floats: the wave equation, the dippers, the 2,000-row least-squares solve (the page's own Cholesky), every guess. The same arithmetic re-run in numpy gives the same bits. What is not real: the water. Real water has surface tension, splashes that add volume, big waves that are not linear, evaporation and noise, all of which troubled the 2003 bucket. The picture of the surface is the true height field through a contrast curve, and the lamps glow on a log scale.

What is this?

The box. Cornell's Program of Computer Graphics first rendered a box like this for Goral, Torrance, Greenberg and Battaile's 1984 radiosity paper, Modeling the interaction of light between diffuse surfaces: an empty box, with a blue right wall. Occluding blocks came with Cohen and Greenberg's hemi-cube in 1985. Cornell later built the box and published its measurements: every corner in millimetres ("measured from the physical Cornell Box", so the walls are not quite square), the reflectance of the white, red and green paint every 4 nm from 400 to 700 nm, the lamp's emission spectrum at four wavelengths, and the camera (a 35 mm lens and 25 mm film at 278, 273, −800 mm). This page uses exactly those numbers, and nothing else, for the box.

How the picture is made. Monte Carlo path tracing, which estimates Kajiya's 1986 rendering equation. Every frame, each pixel sends one or more new paths into the box from the camera, on your GPU (a WebGL2 fragment shader, 32-bit floats). At every surface the path picks a random point on the lamp and adds its light if nothing is in the way (next-event estimation), then leaves in a random direction weighted by the cosine to the surface, as a matte surface scatters light. A path ends at the bounce limit, or, with “∞”, by Russian roulette from the fifth bounce on, which keeps the average exact. The picture is the average of all paths so far, so it starts as grain and settles.

Colour. Each path carries four wavelengths spread across 400–700 nm (hero-wavelength sampling, Wilkie et al. 2014), so every bounce multiplies measured spectra, not RGB triples. The result is turned into CIE 1931 XYZ, white-balanced to the lamp like a camera (Bradford), and shown in sRGB through a filmic tone curve (Narkowicz's ACES fit). “White bal. off” shows the lamp's real, warmer colour, as in Cornell's own picture. The lamp spectrum between Cornell's four values is a straight line, and wavelengths outside 400–700 nm, where Cornell gives no data, are left out. The other paints are the 24 measured patches of a ColorChecker chart (Ohta 1997).

Noise. The paths are split into two independent halves; half their difference is an honest estimate of the error left in the full average, with no reference picture needed. On the machine this page was built on, the error fell from 16 to 16,384 paths per pixel with a fitted slope of −0.500 on log–log axes, against −0.5 for 1/√N; at 16,384 paths it was 0.71%. Halving the noise always costs four times the paths.

The white furnace. Make every surface glow with the same brightness E and reflect 80% of what lands on it, and close the box with a front wall that only bounced light can see. Then every path, whatever it hits, collects exactly E(1 + 0.8 + 0.8² + …), so after k reflections every pixel must read E(1 − 0.8k+1)/(1 − 0.8), the blocks disappear, and there is no noise at all. The GPU gives that for all 380,104 pixels inside the box (in a 640 × 640 picture) at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 64 bounces (the furnace's “∞”), the worst one off by 3×10−5 (single-precision rounding over 1,024 paths). Building this page, the test caught two bugs: paths slipping out through the seams between walls when rounding put a hit point a ten-thousandth of a millimetre outside the box, and a tour layout that pushed the short block through the red wall.

Cross-checks. A separate path tracer written in Python with 64-bit floats, sharing only the data, was compared with the GPU over 44 patches of 8 × 8 pixels in four set-ups (all bounces, one, three, and the tour's moved block with a blue wall), in X, Y, Z and the mean over wavelengths: 176 comparisons, the largest difference 1.78 standard errors, root-mean-square 0.92, as chance predicts. With one bounce the answer is exact: Lambert's 1760 formula for the light a polygon throws on a point gives the lit red, green and back walls to within 0.08% of the GPU, and the ceiling is exactly 0. Click any pixel to rerun this check on the CPU in double precision.

How much is bounced light? Of the light the walls and blocks send to the camera, about 54% arrives after one bounce, 78% within two and 89% within three; the rest needs more.

What is simulated. The surfaces are perfectly matte (Lambertian), as Cornell's data assumes, the lamp is a flat diffuse emitter of reflectance 0.78, and the camera is an ideal pinhole with no lens fall-off. Cornell also photographed the real box through seven narrow-band filters with a cooled CCD; those photographs are not shown here.

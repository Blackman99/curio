What is this?

A 20 cm square of glass, 1.1 mm thick, strewn with sand. A violin bow drives one point of its edge at the pitch on the dial. Wherever the glass accelerates faster than gravity the sand hops; it can only come to rest where the glass moves less than 1 g. Ernst Chladni showed these figures in 1787, bowing glass plates and writing down the note each figure sang. The plate here is sized so that its lowest note is a G (98.3 Hz), like his.

Computed, the way Ritz did it. In 1909 Walther Ritz calculated Chladni's figures for the first time, and invented a method to do it: write the plate's bending energy (Kirchhoff's) for a sum of products of free–free beam shapes um(x)·vn(y), and minimise it. The page does exactly that in your browser, with 16 × 16 = 256 products and Poisson's ratio 0.225 (Ritz's value for glass): Gauss–Legendre integrals, then a Jacobi eigen-solver (7–8 sweeps). LAPACK on the same matrices agrees to 4 × 10−13. A separate Ritz solution with 576 Legendre polynomials (Python) puts the page's first 60 eigenvalues 0.04%–0.45% high (Ritz's method can only err upward), and both agree with Gander & Kwok's finite-difference table to within 0.4%.

Ritz's table and Chladni's ear. The 35 figures in the panel are the rows of Ritz's Tabelle der Tonhöhen. The 9 he worked through fully by hand are within 2.0% of this page; for the rest he kept one term and is up to 11% high (λ = 2,020 for u2v4 + u4v2). His sixth row is printed u1v1; it can only be u2v2. Chladni named 32 of the notes by ear: 18 are within half a semitone of the page's pitch and 30 within a semitone. He was mostly flat (27 of 32), at worst by 2.0 semitones.

Same note, different figures. When m + n is odd, umvn and unvm ring at exactly the same pitch (here they agree to the last bit), and any mixture of the two is also a mode. So where you bow picks the figure, and a fingertip, modelled as pinning one point of the glass to zero, picks the one whose line runs under it.

What is modelled, and how loosely. The response is the sum of all 253 bending modes, each with a quality factor of 1,000 (assumed), driven by a steady force at the bow; the plate's rigid motions are left out. The sand is a simple rule, not ballistics: each tick, a grain where the glass moves faster than 1 g hops a random step of 2 mm × (0.35 + √(a − 1)), and grains that go over the edge fall onto the table. That is enough to make the lines and set their width: on the diagonals the median grain ends 5.0 mm from the line at bow pressure 12, 2.2 mm at 25 and 0.9 mm at 50. A real bow picks its own pitch by stick and slip (here you set it); Chladni clamped his plates at the centre (here, as in Ritz's paper, the plate is free); the sound is a plain sine at the dial's pitch. The engine and a Python reference agree bit for bit, grain by grain.

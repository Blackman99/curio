Quantization Mosaic

A small network learned to paint one blue-and-white tile. Its 784 weights are the tiles of the wall beneath each painting. Store them in fewer bits and the workshop has fewer stamps to choose from: every weight on the wall is set with the nearest one. Five copies of the same network, side by side; the cracks mark where a copy paints the tile differently from the 32-bit one.

Fire them again. Put the four lower walls back in the kiln: retrain each one with its own stamps in the loop, the weights nudged underneath while every painting is done with the stamps.

What is this?

The painter

A network with 32 inputs, two hidden layers of 16 ReLU units and one output (784 weights, 33 biases) learned to paint this tile: at every point it says ink or glaze. It only sees one eighth of the tile, the point folded into the triangle 0 ≤ v ≤ u ≤ 1, through 32 cosines of the folded position (Fourier features), so whatever it paints keeps the tile's eight-fold symmetry. It was trained by this page's own code: full-batch Adam, learning rate 0.01, 3,000 steps in float64, on the 1,176 pixel centres of a 96 × 96 grid that fall in the triangle, seed 7. Its 32-bit copy matches the design on 98.6% of a 128 × 128 grid (30 seeds: median 98.5%, lowest 98.0%). The design is an original one in the blue-and-white manner, drawn for this page.

The walls

Each wall is the same network with its weights stored in fewer bits. Each of a wall's three panels (layer 1: 16 rows of 32; layer 2: 16 × 16, two units to a row; the 16 output weights along the bottom) gets one set of 2b stamps, evenly spaced across ± the largest weight in that panel, and every weight is set with the nearest stamp. The quantizer is symmetric and uniform with no zero stamp, so even one bit keeps the sign. The 32-bit wall is IEEE single precision. A white tile is a positive weight and a blue tile a negative one; the diamond grows with the size. Biases stay 32-bit on every wall (33 numbers, 132 bytes, not counted in the byte totals). Only the weights are rounded: the arithmetic is float64 throughout. This is weight-only quantization, the kind used to run large language models on small machines; integer hardware also rounds the activations.

Rounding

Rounded straight from the trained weights, seed 7 repaints 0.15% of the tile at 8 bits, 2.0% at 4, 7.6% at 2 and 12.5% at 1. Repainted means the share of a 128 × 128 grid where the wall says ink and the 32-bit wall says glaze, or the other way round. Over 30 seeds the medians are 0.15%, 2.2%, 9.4% and 12.3% (1 bit: 8.1% to 17.1%).

The kiln

Re-firing is quantization-aware training. Each lower wall is trained again for 600 rounds of Adam (learning rate 0.01, cosine-decayed to zero) with its stamps in the forward pass. Rounding has no useful gradient, so the gradient is passed straight through to hidden float weights underneath, which are rounded again before the next round (the straight-through estimator). The biases are retrained too. After 600 rounds seed 7 repaints 0.63%, 2.4% and 4.3% at 4, 2 and 1 bits. Over 30 seeds the median 1-bit wall goes from 12.3% to 3.5%, and in 30 of 30 seeds the re-fired 1-bit wall repaints less than the rounded 2-bit wall. The re-fired 2-bit wall beats the rounded 4-bit wall in 15 of 30. The 8-bit wall had nothing to mend: re-fired it ends at a median 0.20% against 0.15% rounded, worse in 19 of 30.

Why the curve jitters

Weights that sit near the boundary between two stamps hop back and forth. Over its 600 rounds the 1-bit wall of seed 7 changes a stamp 2,329 times, yet only 115 of its 784 tiles end on a different stamp from the one they started with (30 seeds: medians 2,366 and 91). This oscillation is a known side effect of the straight-through estimator. The slider shows every tenth round as it was, jitter included, so the best moment is often a little better than the end (median gap at 1 bit: 0.40%).

Computed, and drawn

All five paintings are the networks' own outputs, evaluated on a 128 × 128 grid and drawn with a one-pixel edge taken from the slope of the output. The cracks are drawn wherever a wall's painting differs from the 32-bit wall's; the glaze texture is decoration. The page's engine uses its own exp and cos series, a fixed summation order and a counter-based random generator, and a numpy twin matches it bit for bit: the 3,000-step training, all four 600-round kilns and every evaluated painting. Train a new painter trains a fresh one from another seed in a worker, here in your browser, and fires its kiln.

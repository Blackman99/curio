What is this?

The idea. A balance wheel is never perfectly poised: one side is a little heavier. Lying flat that does nothing, but stood on edge, gravity pulls the heavy spot and the watch gains or loses depending on where the spot happens to sit. Abraham-Louis Breguet’s answer, patented on 26 June 1801 (7 Messidor, year IX), was to put the whole escapement in a cage that turns once a minute, so the spot points every way in turn and the error averages out (Breguet). He built it for pocket watches, which spend the day upright.

What is computed. Every part is generated in this page: 116 of them (wheels with cycloidal-style teeth, Geneva-striped bridges traced from distance fields, jewels, blued screws, a balance with 16 gold timing screws, a hairspring that coils and uncoils with the swing). Every wheel turns by its tooth count: barrel 96 → centre pinion 12, centre wheel 80 → third pinion 10, third wheel 75 → cage pinion 10, so the cage turns 60 times an hour and is the seconds hand; the escape pinion (7) rolls round a fixed wheel (112), giving 16 escape-wheel turns per cage turn, 28,800 beats an hour. Centre distances are module × (z1 + z2)/2 and every pair starts in mesh. The mainspring is sized by the old rule (arbor a third of the barrel, spring filling half the free space): 6.81 turns, 54.5 hours.

The heavy spot. The balance’s inertia, 4.55 mg·cm², comes from the modelled rim, arm and screws. A heavy spot U stood on edge adds a torque −U g sin(θ + φ); averaging over one swing of amplitude A gives a rate of 86 400 · U g/(I ω02) · cos φ · J1(A)/A seconds a day. J1 crosses zero at 219.5°: above it a spot at the bottom loses, below it the same spot gains, which is why watchmakers say poise errors vanish near 220°. I checked the formula against a direct Runge–Kutta integration of the balance: at 5 µg·cm it agrees to within 0.006 s/day from 150° to 305°. The page runs beat by beat: each beat lasts 1/8 s ÷ (1 + rate/86 400), with the rate taken where the cage stands at that beat.

What the tourbillon does. Crown up, 5 µg·cm, full wind (281° upright): held still with the spot at the bottom the watch runs −9.46 s/day; turning, the 480 beats of one cage turn average −0.0005 s/day (the direct integration over two whole turns gives +0.01 s/day). Let the spring run down and the upright amplitude passes 219.5° with 16 hours left; below that the held error flips sign. Dial up or down, gravity runs along the staff and there is nothing to cancel.

What is simplified. The tooth counts are my own design for a 28,800-beat, one-minute tourbillon, not copied from a real calibre. Within a beat, the lever, escape wheel and cage move along a drawn path (unlock, impulse, drop); the beat times are what is computed. Amplitude is a simple model: torque falls to 45% as the spring runs down, amplitude goes with its square root, and upright positions lose 8%. Isochronism and escapement errors are left out. “Held” is a thought experiment: in a real tourbillon, stopping the cage stops the watch. The regulator moves curb pins along the outer coil; rate follows f ∝ L−½ (16 s/day per degree).

Rose Window

A twelve-metre rose window lays its colours on the floor whenever the sun comes round. Scroll to draw one with a compass, glaze it, and follow its light through a day.

Sunlight

The sun is not a point

If it were, the floor would carry a sharp copy of every pane, lead lines and all. It is half a degree wide, so each point of the glass lands as a soft disc 28 cm across, and only the big shapes survive.

Drawing · 1

One circle, then the radius six times

A rose is drawn before it is cut: full size, on a plaster tracing floor. Keep the compass at the radius and step it round the rim. It fits exactly six times.

Drawing · 2

Halve, and halve again

Two arcs struck from neighbouring points cross on the line that halves them. Halving the six steps gives the twelve spokes; halving again finds the middle of each sector.

Drawing · 3

The circle that touches three lines

The tangent at the middle of the rim cuts both spokes into a triangle. Bisect one corner: the bisector crosses the middle line at the centre of the only circle that touches both spokes and the rim. That is Euclid, Book IV, proposition 4.

Drawing · 4

Walk it round, then shrink it

The same moves give all twelve petals. The ring inside is the same figure turned half a sector and shrunk by 0.589, the number fixed by twelve: (1 − sin 15°) / (1 + sin 15°).

Drawing · 5

Foils

Inside a petal the trick runs again with four spokes instead of twelve. Four circles, each touching its neighbours and the petal, make a quatrefoil.

Drawing · 6

Filling the gaps

Each gap left between petals takes the one circle touching three others, Apollonius's problem. A six-lobed eye goes in the middle, and the drawing is done.

What is this?

A rose window twelve metres across, its centre twenty metres up a south-facing wall at the latitude of Chartres. It is not a copy of any real window: the tracery is drawn by the page with compass and straightedge, and the glass is a made-up mosaic of medieval colours. The sunlight is computed for the real sky.

The drawing is real geometry

The page's construction engine knows two moves, a circle through a point and a line through two points, and how to intersect them. From one circle it steps out the hexagon (or the square, or the decagon, whose side is R(√5 − 1)/2, Euclid XIII.9), halves arcs, draws the tangent at the middle of a sector and bisects a corner of the triangle it makes (Euclid IV.4) to get each petal, then repeats inside the petals for the foils. A separate closed form gives the same circles directly: a circle inscribed in a sector of angle 2π/n has radius R·sin(π/n)/(1 + sin(π/n)) and its centre at R/(1 + sin(π/n)). For 6 to 24 petals, 1 to 3 rings and 3, 4 or 6 foils the two agree on 2,865 centres and radii to 1.6×10⁻14 m. With 12 petals each ring is the one outside scaled by 0.5888.

The small circles in the gaps touch three others (Apollonius's problem). They are placed by formula, not by construction; the page's JavaScript and an independent Python version agree, and every tangency holds to 4×10⁻¹⁵ m. Only constructible petal counts are offered: by the Gauss–Wantzel theorem a 7- or 9-fold rose cannot be drawn with these two tools.

The sun

Sun positions use NOAA's solar calculation (the Global Monitoring Laboratory's spreadsheet, after Jean Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms), including its refraction model, with the city's time zone and daylight saving taken from the browser. Checked against JPL Horizons (ephemeris DE441) at six sites from Quito to Reykjavík on seven dates from 1250 to 2099: 1,789 positions with the sun above the horizon, worst 0.016°, median 0.0038°. For 2026 to 2099 the worst is 0.0101°, one 53th of the sun's width. The sun's disc is 2 × 959.63″ divided by its distance in astronomical units.

The light on the floor

Nothing is painted on the floor. Every pixel of floor looks back toward 40 points spread evenly over the sun's disc; each of those rays has to clear the inside edge of the 1.2-m-thick wall, and then takes the colour of the glass where it crosses the window. So the patch is the window sheared and stretched by the angle of the sun, and blurred by the sun's own width: at 14:40 on 16 April the glass is 25.9 m from its patch and each point of it lands as a disc 24 cm across. The beams in the air use the same test at points along each line of sight.

Checked: the page reads back the floor light the GPU computed on a grid across the patch, and the glass texture it sampled, and a separate float64 version in Python recomputes it. Of 57,600 floor points, 57,500 agree to 10⁻⁴ of full sunlight (median difference 9×10⁻⁹); at the other 100 a ray lands on the far side of a texel edge in single precision, worst 2.3% of the brightest point. The light is also conserved: summed over the floor on a 423×337 grid, the patch carries what the glass lets through to within 0.05% in each colour.

A sundial nobody meant to build

On an equinox the sun's rays all lie in one plane, parallel to the equator, so the centre of the patch, where that plane meets the floor, runs along a straight line. For a south wall the line is parallel to the wall at 20 m × tan(latitude) minus the wall's thickness: 21.4 m at Chartres. On 20 March 2026 the centre travels 220 m and strays from a straight line by 10 cm, because the sun's declination drifts 0.16° during the 10.0 hours it shines through. Other days trace hyperbolas: on 21 June the patch stays 4–8 m from the wall and is lit for 6.4 hours; on 21 December it lands 60 m out at noon.

What is left out

  • The sun is a uniform disc (no limb darkening) in a clear sky; light does not bounce, so the stone is lit by a constant ambient level.
  • The glass is a thin sheet with made-up colours; the depth of the tracery bars is ignored, only the wall's thickness clips the beam. The floor goes on for ever.
  • Beams and dust are drawn with the same light test but a made-up haze density.

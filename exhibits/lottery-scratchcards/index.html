What is this?

The ticket. One network: 64 pixels, 40 hidden ReLU units, 10 digits, 2,960 wires plus 50 biases that are never cut. Each small patch is one hidden unit’s 64 incoming wires laid out like the 8×8 image; the tall block on the right is the 400 wires from units to digits. Dot size is the wire’s size, red is positive, blue is negative.

The ritual. This is iterative magnitude pruning from Frankle & Carbin’s The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (ICLR 2019). Train for 600 Adam steps (batch 32, rate 0.003). Cut the smallest 20% of the live wires in the first set and 10% in the second, as they do for their MNIST network. Put the survivors back to their starting numbers. Repeat. Their Lenet used a rate of 0.0012 and far more training; the steps and the rate here are mine, chosen so a whole strip fits in a few seconds.

The digits are real handwriting: UCI Optical Digits (Alpaydin & Kaynak, 1998), bitmaps counted down to 8×8. Train on 1,500 digits from the file written by 30 people, score on 1,000 from the file written by 13 others, both spaced evenly through the published files. To fit the page, pixels are stored in 4 bits, so a full count of 16 becomes 15.

What 30 draws say (seeds 1–30, medians; the default seed 7 is not picked for its result). Full ticket 94.7%. At 20% of the wires 93.9%, and the last ticket within one point of the full one has 18.6% of the wires. At 5% (147 wires): own numbers 89.8%, new numbers 86.0%, random holes 54.9%, and own numbers beat new numbers in 30 of 30. At 3.6%: 87.2% against 80.2%. After 25 steps the full ticket has 67.0% and the 20% ticket 87.8%: it starts ahead.

Signs. Zhou, Lan, Liu & Yosinski (Deconstructing Lottery Tickets, NeurIPS 2019) found the sign is what matters in the rewind. Here too: at 5%, same signs with new sizes 89.5%, same sizes with new signs 85.4%, and signs beat sizes in 29 of 30. 71% of the full ticket’s wires end training with the sign they started with; 97% of the 5% ticket’s do.

Never scratched. Also from Zhou et al., who report 86% on MNIST with their best masks. Here the holes alone, on untrained numbers with biases at zero, peak at a median of 81.8% (74.7%–86.4% across seeds), around a fifth of the wires; the same numbers with no holes score 10.2%, about chance.

Punch rate. Gentle wins, as in the original. At about 8% of the wires: 92.0% at 20% a round, 89.0% at 80%. At 3.6%: 87.2% at 20% a round, 84.7% at 50%, 72.8% at 80%.

Raw ink. With pixels from 0 to 1 and no centring, a unit whose wires are mostly negative may never fire. At 5%, new numbers start with 18 of 40 units dead, its own numbers with 9, and the gap widens: 88.5% against 78.9%. Centred, both start with 4 dead and the gap is 89.8% against 86.0%. Flipping every sign is 61.5% on raw ink and 89.0% centred (not on the page), so part of what looks like lucky signs is keeping units alive, and centring takes that part away.

The limits. A two-layer network on 8×8 digits is the easy case. Frankle & Carbin needed a lower learning rate or warmup on their deeper networks, and Frankle et al. (2020) rewind to an early training step rather than the very start on large ones. A ticket is also only known to be a winner after the full network has been trained and cut, so none of this saves the first training.

Real and not. Every training run, cut and score happens in your browser in float64, and is checked against a separate numpy version, weight for weight. The coin is theatre: scratching plays back a run that was already computed, so the foil coming off tracks training steps, it does not cause them. The prize, the WINNER stamp (within one point of the full ticket) and the dots are the run’s real numbers. Paper, foil and coin are drawn in three.js. The 30-draw numbers come from the page’s own engine, run in Node.

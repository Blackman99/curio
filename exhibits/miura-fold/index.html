Miura FoldA solar array that opens with one pull

Eighty panels, one motion.

What is this?

Miura Fold

Every panel on this drawing is the same parallelogram, and every crossing joins four creases at the same four angles. In 1985 Koryo Miura of Japan's Institute of Space and Astronautical Science (ISAS) showed that a sheet creased like this folds in two perpendicular directions at once, as a single motion: “the deformation given to a particular fundamental region will propagate over the entire surface in an instant.” ISAS flew the idea as the 2D Array experiment on the Space Flyer Unit (launched 1995): a membrane stowed in Miura-ori and deployed “by simply pulling the ends of the stowed surface”.

The array here is a generic one: 10 × 8 panels with straight edges ξ = 0.50 m and zigzag edges 0.45 m, the zigzag leaning θ = 6° off square, the value Miura plotted. Flat it measures 5.05 × 3.58 m.

What you can do

  • Drag the orange corner, or any crossing of creases. One corner is bolted to the root fitting. Whatever you hold can only move along its own track (the orange dashed line for the corner): the sheet has one degree of freedom, so the page finds the fold angle ω that puts the crossing closest to your pointer.
  • Crease skew θ. 0° is a plain grid, like a road map. 6° is Miura's figure. Try 20°.
  • Panels changes the sheet size; Lines / Panels switch between the drawing (hidden edges dashed) and hardware with cells and hinges. Stow and Deploy run the whole motion. Drag empty space to turn the view, scroll to zoom, double-click to reset.

What is computed

Miura's equations (1)–(2): when every panel stands at angle ω to the original plane, the sheet shortens to Sx = cos[sin−1(cos θ sin ω)] along the straight creases and Sy = cos ω / cos[sin−1(cos θ sin ω)] across them. Crossing (i, j) then sits at x = i ξ Sx + V·[j odd], y = j η Sy, z = H·[i odd], with η = 0.45 cos θ, V = 0.45 sin θ / Sx and H = ξ sin ω cos θ. The Locus chart is that pair (Sx, Sy); with an even number of panels each way it is exactly the path of the free corner.

Every frame the page re-measures every panel from the vertices it draws: both edge lengths, the corner angle, and how far the fourth corner is from the plane of the other three. Worst so far in this visit: —.

Checked against an independent fold. A Python script starts from the flat crease pattern and does not use the formulas above: it finds the fold angles at one crossing by requiring the four rotations about its creases to close up, then turns panel after panel about its creases. In 66 configurations (θ 0.5–30°, ω 5–89.5°) its sheet matches the page's to 3×10−13 m after a rigid move; across all 189 configurations the page's engine matches a numpy transcription of the equations to 7×10−13 m.

Checked against Miura's Fig. 8. The open circles in the Locus chart are the 13 points read off the scanned figure (θ = 6°, ω = 0°–90°). After calibrating the axes, the largest gap to the equations is 0.005 of full scale, about the width of a printed dot.

What surprised me

One motion, but a lopsided one. With θ = 6°, the last 10° of ω before folded flat take Y from 0 to 86% of its length while X only goes from 10% to 20%; 44% of the corner's whole journey happens there. Miura describes the same “two distinct phases”. The creases show why: the zigzag creases always fold at exactly 2ω, but the straight creases are only at 11.9° when the zigzags reach 90°, and 61° when they reach 160°. One family is nearly closed before the other has started.

So θ sets how sharp the turn is. At 1° the sheet still has 99.5% of its Y length at ω = 80°; at 0° the turn becomes a corner and the second direction needs a second, separate fold, the road-map problem Miura was escaping. At 20° Y is already down to 46% at ω = 80°: the two directions travel together.

What is not real

  • The kinematics are for panels of zero thickness. The drawn panels are 8 mm plates set 12 mm back from every crease, and the motion stops when any two panels come within 2.5° of each other (ω = 88.75°), so the stowed sheet here is a 0.97 × 0.73 × 0.50 m block rather than a flat stack. Real thick panels need offset hinges.
  • The cells, hinges and root fitting are illustrative. They are not the hardware of the 2D Array, which was a membrane.
  • Needs WebGL. Works in current Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari.

What is this?

The machine. The parts follow the Mark I Perceptron Operators’ Manual (Hay, Lynch & Smith, Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, 1960): 400 photoresistors in a 20×20 array, a 20×20 neon monitor with a 20×20 switch array below it, excitatory and inhibitory plugboards with 20 sockets per cell, 512 A-units eight to a card, eight R-units. An A-unit’s relay closes when its lit excitatory cords outnumber its lit inhibitory cords by the threshold θ (1 to 7). Each one’s potentiometer then adds between −11 and +11 volts to its R-unit. An IBM 704 printed the random wiring, up to 15 excitatory and 15 inhibitory cords per unit; the manual says starting number “1” had been used, so this page starts at 1 too, with a different generator. The shafts turn about 1/16 of a revolution a minute at full feedback, and a reset takes about twenty minutes.

The rule. Section 7.5, “error correction”: show a slide; if the response is wrong, feed back to the A-units that fired until it flips. Novikoff (1962) proved the number of mistakes is finite whenever some setting of the knobs gets the whole tray right. Rosenblatt set out the theory in 1958.

The ladder. Over 60 random wirings with an 80-slide tray, tested on 200 slides it never saw. Left or right: median 8.5 mistakes, 98.2% right (93–100%); after only 50 slides, 96.8% (200 wirings). E or X anywhere on the retina: 72.5 mistakes, 70.5% (62.5–77%), rising to 86.2% with 320 slides. One piece or two: 77 mistakes, 50.5% (44–57%), and still 52.5% with 320 slides and 50.0% with 1,280. Shuffling the labels costs 83 mistakes on that tray but 1,696 on the left/right one: to this machine “connected” looks like noise. Both classes carry the same ink (52.0 and 52.1 lit cells), so there is nothing to count.

XOR. With no hidden layer (X = 1, Y = 0, θ = 1) the knobs would need wL > 0, wR > 0 and wL + wR ≤ 0, which is impossible; 12 of 12 runs go 3 of 4 wrong on every pass and return to the same setting. With the random layer: median 3 mistakes, 60 of 60 solved. The layer is not trained and buys capacity, not insight: with no hidden layer it scores 99.5%, 67.8% and 51.5% on the three unseen tests above. Minsky and Papert (1969) analysed machines that do have A-units, and proved that the order needed for parity and for connectedness grows with the retina. The coin flip here is an experiment on this wiring, not their proof.

The wires. Cutting 25, 50, 75 or 90% of the cords after left/right training leaves 97.5, 94.0, 85.5 or 66.8% right. Half cut, 3 more mistakes bring it to 97.0%. Re-patching with the knobs left alone gives 52.8%, and relearning takes 9 mistakes against 8 from scratch.

Real and not. Every firing, knob and score is computed in your browser, in integers, and matches a separate numpy implementation bit for bit. These are my choices, because the manual doesn’t fix them: X 10, Y 5, θ 2; all 512 units plugged to R1; a knob’s travel of 16 ticks, taken as a 300° sweep, so one tick is 18.75° or 50 s of motor. Machine time adds 4 s a slide, the projector timer’s minimum. The manual’s “few millivolts” threshold is “above zero” here. The slides are generated: left/right echoes the 1958 demonstration, which ran on an IBM 704 before the Mark I was built in 1959; E or X and one piece or two are my own, and no recorded Mark I experiment is being replayed. The look is an impression from the manual’s descriptions: the coloured knob caps, retina-shaped plugboards, chart and counters are mine. The real machine is at the Smithsonian.

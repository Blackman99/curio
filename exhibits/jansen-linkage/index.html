What is this?

A walking machine in the manner of Theo Jansen's beach animals: one crankshaft, and on every crank throw a leg and its mirror image. Each leg is the frame (a, l), the crank (m) and ten rods (b–k). Every joint sits where two circles meet, so one turn of the crank is five circle intersections per angle, computed here 120 times a turn for the score and every frame for the 3D model.

How Jansen found his numbers

In his own words on strandbeest.com: “Fifteen hundred legs with rods of random length were generated in the computer. It then assessed which of these approached the ideal walking curve. Out of the 1500, the computer selected the best 100. These were awarded the privilege of reproduction.” The ideal was a curve with a flat base and rounded corners: while the foot is on the base it carries the animal along a straight line, like the axle of a wheel, so “the beach animal doesn't lurch.” The result, after weeks of computing in 1990, was his holy numbers: a 38, b 41.5, c 39.3, d 40.1, e 55.8, f 39.4, g 36.7, h 65.7, i 49, j 50, k 61.9, l 7.8, m 15.

How a leg is scored here

Jansen never published his yardstick, so this page uses its own, built from his description:

  • The beast. With 2 × P legs it has P crank throws spread evenly round the crank. The body rides on its lowest foot and moves with that foot without slipping.
  • Stride is how far the body travels in one turn of the crank. Lurch is the furthest the body strays from rolling straight at constant speed, up–down or forward–back, in cm.
  • Walk score = stride ÷ (1 + lurch / 0.5 cm), scaled down if the feet lift less than 15 cm or more than 45 cm.
  • A leg scores 0 if any joint cannot close or comes within about 3° of folding flat (where real linkages jam), if the foot meets the sand less than 85 or more than 100 cm below the crank, or if a knee comes within 5 cm of the sand.

Jansen's leg on 12 legs: the crank sits 91.8 cm above the sand, the feet lift 22.5 cm, the body moves 141 cm a turn and lurches 0.24 cm (0.08 up–down): score 96. Its weakest beast is the 6-legged one (39).

The evolution on this page

Jansen's recipe with small numbers filled in: 1,500 legs with rods drawn at random between 20 and 80 cm (frame and crank stay his); the best 100 breed; each child takes every rod from one of two parents and nudges 30% of them by a random amount (spread 0.3 or 2 cm); the best leg passes on unchanged. One press runs 60 generations. Breed for this beast scores legs on the beast now on the beach; every beast scores a leg by its worst result on the 6- to 24-legged beasts.

What 30 runs show

Node ran this page's engine for 30 seeds in each mode, 60 generations each.

  • Of 1,500 random legs, a median of 41 (between 28 and 55) can turn the crank and walk at all.
  • Bred for the 12-legged beast, the best leg passes Jansen's 96 by generation 2.5 (median; every run by 5) and ends at 128 (107–159). Moved to the other beasts, 16 of the 30 do worse somewhere than Jansen's leg does on its weakest beast; on 6 legs the median lurch is 1.6 cm and the worst 44 cm.
  • Bred for every beast, all 30 beat Jansen's weakest beast (39): median 53. On 12 legs they reach a median of 88, and 11 of 30 pass his 96.
  • No run comes back to his numbers. The rods of the best legs differ from his by 13 cm on average (median; the closest run 6.2 cm): many quite different legs walk.

So the evolution does what it is told. A leg bred for one beast is fitted to that beast; Jansen's leg, bred for the shape of one foot path, walks every beast here between 39 and 96.

What is real, what is not

  • Computed: the linkage (Jansen's published lengths, five circle intersections), the gait, the score and the evolution, all in 64-bit floats with a fixed order of operations and a counted random generator. A numpy reference reproduces them bit for bit: 3,780 values including whole evolution runs, plus the page's own logs.
  • Not modelled: forces, weight, balance (the body never tips), sand, and the wind: the crank simply turns at 0.4 turns a second. The arrangement of the legs (evenly spread throws, each with a mirrored pair) is this page's, not a copy of any of Jansen's animals.
  • Sizes are true to scale in cm; the 3D beast is drawn from the same numbers as the score.

Make one and keep it

Copy link puts the ten rod lengths and the number of legs in the address; anyone who opens it sees your leg on that beast. Save card downloads a picture of the leg, its foot path and its numbers. Tweak rods lets you change one rod at a time and see the score move.
